# What Is Curriculum Compacting?

If your child keeps saying school is "too easy," curriculum compacting is one of the first things worth asking their teacher about. The idea is refreshingly simple: skip the material a child has already mastered, and give that time back for something new and more challenging. Instead of sitting through a unit they could practically teach themselves, they test out of it and move on. It was built for exactly this kind of learner, and the research is honestly a little startling: teachers have trimmed 40 to 50% of the usual curriculum for high-ability students without their scores dropping at all.

## How it works
It really comes down to three moves:

1. See what the child already knows, usually with a quick pretest.
2. Skip the lessons and practice for whatever they've already got down.
3. Give that time back for something better: more advanced material, a deeper project, or enrichment.

It's easy to hear "compacting" and picture a kid getting less. It's the opposite. You're just refusing to make them relearn what they already know, and spending that time on work that's actually worth it.

## Why it matters for gifted kids
Here's the part that catches a lot of parents off guard: for many bright kids, a big slice of the school year is review they simply don't need. In the 1993 Curriculum Compacting Study, a national study of about 436 teachers across 27 districts, teachers removed up to half of the regular curriculum for high-ability students with no drop in achievement, and the compacted math and science groups actually scored higher. In that same study, 78 to 88% of the strongest 5th and 6th grade readers passed tests on skills before those skills had even been taught.

[CHART: two research numbers side by side, "40 to 50% of the curriculum trimmed with no loss in achievement" and "78 to 88% of top readers passed pretests before instruction" (NRC/GT, 1993)]

Sit with that for a second. Without something like compacting, a capable kid can spend much of the year just waiting, and that waiting is usually where the boredom and checking-out that parents notice actually begins.

## What it looks like
Picture a fourth grader who already has multi-digit multiplication down cold, and a quick pretest confirms it. Instead of three more weeks of worksheets she doesn't need, her teacher compacts the unit and hands that time back: maybe an early start on fractions, maybe a data project, maybe a stack of tougher problems to sink her teeth into. Same classroom, same schedule. The only thing that changes is how high she's allowed to reach.

## A few related terms
- [Academic acceleration](does-grade-skipping-hurt-kids-socially): moving a child up to higher-level content or a higher grade (often paired with compacting).
- Mastery-based learning: advancing once material is truly mastered, instead of by seat-time.
- [Under-challenge](is-my-gifted-child-under-challenged): the quiet problem compacting is designed to solve.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is curriculum compacting?
It's a teaching strategy that removes material a student has already mastered and replaces it with more challenging or enriching work. It was developed for high-ability students who already know much of the grade-level content.

### Does curriculum compacting hurt achievement?
No. In the landmark NRC/GT study, teachers eliminated 40–50% of the regular curriculum for high-ability students with no significant difference in achievement, and compacted math and science groups scored significantly higher.

### Who is curriculum compacting for?
Students who have already mastered a meaningful portion of the grade-level curriculum, often gifted or high-ability learners who would otherwise spend class time reviewing what they already know.

### How is compacting different from acceleration?
Compacting removes already-mastered content within a course so a student can go deeper or faster; acceleration moves a student to higher-level material or grade placement. They're related and often used together.
