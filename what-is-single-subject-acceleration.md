# What Is Single-Subject Acceleration?

Single-subject acceleration means moving a student ahead in just one subject, the one where they are far beyond grade level, while they stay with their age group for everything else. A second grader who has already mastered fourth-grade math, for example, might join a higher grade's math class or work through advanced math online, then rejoin classmates for reading, science, and recess. It is one of the most flexible, lowest-disruption ways to challenge a child who is racing ahead in a single area.

## Key takeaways
- Single-subject acceleration moves a child up in one subject only, keeping age peers for the rest of the day.
- It is one of roughly twenty recognized forms of acceleration.
- Research reviews describe acceleration as a reliable, effective, and inexpensive intervention, endorsed by NAGC.
- It stays underused mostly because of social worries the evidence does not support.

## How it works
The idea is simple: match the level of one subject to what your child is actually ready for, and leave the rest of the day alone. In practice that can look like sending a child to a higher grade for that one class, grouping them with older students for it, or letting them move through a mastery-based online course at their own pace. The child keeps their homeroom, their friends, and their routine for everything else.

## How it differs from grade skipping and compacting
These three often get mixed up, but they solve slightly different problems:

- Grade skipping moves a child up a full grade, across every subject at once.
- [Curriculum compacting](what-is-curriculum-compacting) keeps the child in their grade but removes material they have already mastered, freeing time for something new.
- Single-subject acceleration moves the child to higher-level content in one subject only.

For a child who is dramatically ahead in math but right on track in writing, single-subject acceleration is often the cleanest fit.

## Does it work?
Yes, and the evidence for acceleration in general is strong. A major research review, A Nation Empowered, gathers decades of studies and calls acceleration a reliable, evidence-based intervention that is educationally effective and inexpensive, and it is endorsed by NAGC. Acceleration comes in about twenty forms, and single-subject is one of the gentlest, because the child keeps their social group. The main reason it stays underused is worry about social or emotional effects, which the research generally does not bear out. For the fuller picture, see [does academic acceleration actually work](does-academic-acceleration-actually-work).

## How to ask your school for it
Come prepared and specific. Bring evidence that your child is genuinely ahead in the subject (work samples, a pretest, test scores), ask for single-subject acceleration by name, and propose a short trial so everyone can see how it goes. A brief follow-up email that recaps the plan keeps things on track.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is single-subject acceleration?
Single-subject acceleration means moving a student ahead in just one subject, the one where they are well beyond grade level, while they stay with their age group for everything else. A common example is a child doing math one or more grades up while staying with peers for reading and science.

### How is single-subject acceleration different from grade skipping?
Grade skipping moves a child up a whole grade for every subject. Single-subject acceleration moves them up in only one subject, so they keep their age peers and daily routine for the rest of the day.

### Does single-subject acceleration hurt kids socially?
It is one of the gentlest forms of acceleration, since the child keeps their usual class and friends for most of the day. Research reviews of acceleration overall find the social worries are generally not borne out.

### How do I get single-subject acceleration for my child?
Bring evidence that your child is well ahead in the subject (work samples, test scores), ask the school specifically for single-subject acceleration, and propose a trial period. Putting the request in writing helps.
