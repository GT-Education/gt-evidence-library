How Are Children Tested for Giftedness?

Updated July 2026 · 4 min read

There is no reliable at-home test for giftedness. In practice, a child is identified through a formal evaluation: usually an individually administered cognitive (IQ) test given by a school or a licensed psychologist, often combined with achievement testing, teacher input, and a look at everyday work. The result is a set of percentile scores that show how your child compares to same-age peers, plus context about their specific strengths. Here is how the process usually works and what to expect.

Key takeaways

  • Giftedness is identified by a formal evaluation, not a single at-home checklist.
  • The core tool is an individually administered cognitive test (often the WISC-V), reported as percentiles.
  • Scores should be read against current, non-biased norms, and local norms matter, not just national ones.
  • A single overall score can hide a twice-exceptional child, so ask about the subscores.

Where gifted testing usually starts

There are two common paths. The first is through your child's school: many districts run a group screener or use a teacher referral, then follow up with a fuller evaluation for children who stand out. The second is a private evaluation with a licensed psychologist, which tends to be more in-depth and faster to schedule, but more costly.

One thing to know going in: there is no federal definition of giftedness, so what a "gifted" result means depends on your state and the specific program. Many use roughly the top 10% relative to a national or local norm as a working guide for services.

What the tests actually measure

Cognitive tests like the WISC-V or Stanford-Binet measure intellectual ability and report the result as percentiles or standard scores. They are strong for spotting intellectual giftedness, but they are not as good at capturing creative or leadership talent, so a complete picture often includes achievement tests, work samples, and teacher or parent observations.

Percentile is usually the clearest way to read the result. The 98th percentile means your child scored higher than 98 out of 100 same-age peers. If you want more on the numbers themselves, see what IQ score is considered gifted.

What the process usually looks like

  1. First conversation. Tell the teacher or school counselor what you are seeing, or contact a psychologist who tests for giftedness.
  2. Screening. The school may begin with a group screener or a checklist.
  3. Full evaluation. An individually administered cognitive test, often alongside achievement testing.
  4. Results meeting. You get the scores explained in context, plus a recommendation for services or next steps.

Before the evaluation, it helps to jot down the everyday signs you have been noticing. Concrete examples give the evaluator useful context.

One caution for twice-exceptional kids

If your child is twice-exceptional, meaning gifted and also living with a learning difference like ADHD or dyslexia, a single full-scale score can hide the giftedness, because a learning challenge can pull the overall average down. Ask the evaluator to look at the subscores, not just the composite. If the number and your gut strongly disagree, that gap is worth exploring rather than ignoring.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get my child tested for giftedness?

Start by telling your child's teacher or school counselor what you are noticing and asking about the school's gifted screening process. You can also go straight to a licensed psychologist who evaluates for giftedness. Either way, the core step is an individually administered cognitive test.

What test is used to identify giftedness?

Most evaluations use an individually administered cognitive (IQ) test such as the WISC-V or Stanford-Binet, often paired with achievement tests. Results come back as percentiles or standard scores compared to same-age peers.

At what age can a child be tested for giftedness?

Signs can appear early, but individually administered cognitive tests are generally most reliable from around age 6. A psychologist can advise on the right timing for your child.

Should scores be read against national or local norms?

Both matter. Norms should be current and non-biased, and local norms (how your child compares within their own school or district) can matter as much as national ones, especially for program placement.
