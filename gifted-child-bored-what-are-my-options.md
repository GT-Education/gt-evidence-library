# My Gifted Child Is Bored, What Are My Options?

If your child races through the worksheet and then stares out the window, you're not imagining it, and you have more options than it may feel like right now. They run from small tweaks in the current classroom all the way to a change of school, and there's no single right answer, only the one that fits your kid and your family. The best first move is usually the lowest-effort one: ask the current school to adjust before you overhaul anything.

## Your options, easiest first
Work up the ladder and stop when the fit feels right.

- Adjust in the current classroom. Ask about curriculum compacting (skipping what your child has already mastered) and single-subject acceleration (moving up in just one subject, like math). Both are low-cost and well-supported: in the landmark Curriculum Compacting Study, teachers cut 40 to 50% of the regular curriculum for high-ability kids with no drop in achievement ([NRC/GT, 1993](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED379847.pdf)).
- Grade acceleration. Moving up a full grade suits kids who are ahead across the board and ready for older classmates. The social worry is common but not what the research finds ([A Nation Empowered, 2015](https://www.accelerationinstitute.org/nation_empowered/)); more on that in [does grade-skipping hurt kids socially?](does-grade-skipping-hurt-kids-socially)
- Gifted magnet or pull-out program. A dedicated gifted class, or a few enrichment hours a week, if your district has a strong one. Quality varies a lot, and a few hours may not touch the other 90% of the week.
- Homeschooling. The most flexibility to tailor everything to your child, if you have the time to lead it and can arrange socialization through co-ops, clubs, and sports.
- A full-time gifted school, in person or online. Built so an advanced pace is the default, not the exception. Online solves geography, but check the grade range first: some well-known online gifted schools serve only older students (Davidson Academy Online, for instance, doesn't serve K–5) ([Davidson Academy Online](https://www.davidsononline.org/about-us/who-we-serve/)).

## How to choose
Whatever you're weighing, the same few questions cut through it. Parents tend to trust what they can see and measure; in one national survey, in-person visits and school-rating data were the top research methods, and 57% went with strong data even over a trusted person's negative opinion ([EdChoice, 2024](https://www.edchoice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Parent-Preferences.pdf)). Ask any option:

- How is my child's level set, by mastery or by age?
- How much of the day is genuinely new learning versus review?
- What happens when my child races ahead: does the pace keep up, or is there a ceiling?
- Can I watch a normal day in action?

## Frequently asked questions

### What are my options if my gifted child is bored at school?
You have a ladder, from lowest to highest effort: ask the current school for curriculum compacting or single-subject acceleration, pursue grade acceleration, enroll in a gifted magnet or pull-out program, homeschool, or move to a full-time gifted school (in person or online). The right choice depends on your child and your family's constraints.

### Should I move my gifted child to a new school just because they're bored?
Not necessarily. Start by asking the current school about compacting and acceleration, which the research strongly supports. Change schools when the regular classroom can't flex the pace to match your child.

### Is acceleration a safe option for a bored gifted child?
Yes, for a well-matched child. A Nation Empowered calls acceleration a reliable, evidence-based intervention with positive academic, social, and psychological outcomes, underused because of social worries the evidence doesn't support.

### What online options exist for gifted K–8 students?
Virtual public schools, homeschool-online curricula, and accredited full-time online gifted schools. Confirm grade ranges first, since some well-known online gifted programs start in middle or high school, leaving K–5 families fewer options.
